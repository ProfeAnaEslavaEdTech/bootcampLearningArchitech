Phase 2 · Teach · Framework 1 of 5

DigComp 2.2 — the CEFR for citizens

Audience: everyone — citizens, employees, jobseekers. The EU's reference framework for general digital competence, and the backbone of most publicly funded digital-skills projects.

Anatomy: 5 areas · 21 competences · 8 levels (Foundation 1–2, Intermediate 3–4, Advanced 5–6, Highly specialised 7–8).

The five areas — memorize these

  • 1. Information & data literacy — searching, evaluating, managing
  • 2. Communication & collaboration — interacting, sharing, digital identity
  • 3. Digital content creation — creating, editing, copyright, basic programming
  • 4. Safety — devices, data, wellbeing, environment
  • 5. Problem solving — technical problems, identifying needs, self-development
Talking point: the "2.2" update (2022) added ~250 examples covering AI interaction, remote work, and misinformation — a natural bridge to your AI-in-learning practice.

Quick check: a client says "certify our staff at intermediate level" under DigComp. Which levels are they talking about?

Phase 2 · Teach · Framework 2 of 5

DigCompEdu — your home ground

Audience: teachers, trainers, educators at every level.

Anatomy: 6 areas · 22 competences — and proficiency runs A1 → C2, deliberately modelled on the CEFR: A1 Newcomer, A2 Explorer, B1 Integrator, B2 Expert, C1 Leader, C2 Pioneer. (Yes — the rail above your head.)

The six areas

  • 1. Professional engagement · 2. Digital resources · 3. Teaching & learning
  • 4. Assessment — digital assessment strategies and feedback (your specialty)
  • 5. Empowering learners — accessibility, personalisation (your WCAG/UDL work lives here)
  • 6. Facilitating learners' digital competence
Interview gift: self-assess before any screen. Being able to say "I'd place myself at B2–C1 across areas 2, 4 and 5, and here's the evidence" doesn't just claim framework fluency — it demonstrates it.

Quick check: your DELE assessment design and WCAG/UDL accessibility work map to which two DigCompEdu areas?

Phase 2 · Teach · Framework 3 of 5

e-CF — the European IT standard

Audience: ICT professionals — developers, architects, project managers, security specialists.

Anatomy: 41 competences · 5 areas · levels e-1 → e-5. The areas mirror the ICT business process: Plan → Build → Run → Enable → Manage.

Status detail worth dropping: it is a formal European standard — EN 16234-1 — not just a reference document. Consultancies use it for IT role profiling, job descriptions, and skills-gap analysis.

When a consultant reaches for it: competency mapping for a client's IT department; structuring an IT academy's curriculum.

Quick check: the five e-CF areas follow which logic?

Phase 2 · Teach · Framework 4 of 5

SFIA — the global, seniority-focused one

Audience: IT and digital professionals worldwide. Born in the UK; now the most widely used globally.

Anatomy: ~147 skills, but its signature is the 7 levels of responsibility: 1 Follow → 2 Assist → 3 Apply → 4 Enable → 5 Ensure/Advise → 6 Initiate/Influence → 7 Set strategy.

The conceptual difference: SFIA levels measure autonomy, influence, and complexity — responsibility, not knowledge alone. Two people can share a skill; one applies it (level 3), the other shapes strategy with it (level 6).

CEFR parallel: it's the difference between knowing the grammar and operating in the language professionally. e-CF vs SFIA in one line: e-CF is the European standard organized around ICT processes; SFIA is the global de facto framework organized around skills × responsibility.

Quick check: a client with offices in Milan, São Paulo and Singapore wants ONE framework for its global IT workforce. Which do you shortlist first, and why?

Phase 2 · Teach · Framework 5 of 5

ENISA — spot the trap

The trap in the posting: ENISA is not a framework. It is the EU Agency for Cybersecurity. What it publishes is the ECSF — European Cybersecurity Skills Framework.

Anatomy: 12 role profiles — e.g., CISO, Cyber Incident Responder, Cyber Threat Intelligence Specialist, Cybersecurity Auditor, and — note this one — Cybersecurity Educator. Each profile defines mission, tasks, and required skills/knowledge. Role-based, not levelled.

Why the precision matters: gently distinguishing "ENISA the agency" from "the ECSF it publishes" in conversation signals you've engaged with the source material, not just the acronym list. And the Cybersecurity Educator profile is where your profession intersects it.

Quick check: structurally, how does the ECSF differ from the other four frameworks?

Phase 3 · Test · Application

The consulting scenario

An Italian regional government hires HSPI for a digital upskilling programme with three strands. Choose the anchor framework for each — out loud, before revealing. This is interview simulation.

(a) Assess general digital skills of 800 administrative employees

Reveal reasoning (+10 XP first time)

DigComp 2.2. General workforce, publicly funded EU context, levelled assessment against the EU reference framework. Deliverable: assessment mapped to the 21 competences with level placement 1–8.

(b) Upskill the 40 trainers in the internal academy

Reveal reasoning (+10 XP first time)

DigCompEdu. Educator-specific; its A1–C2 self-assessment logic makes trainer development measurable and motivating. Deliverable: baseline self-assessment, then targeted paths per area.

(c) Profile the competences needed in the 15-person IT department

Reveal reasoning (+10 XP first time)

e-CF — the European standard for ICT role profiling. Mention SFIA as the alternative if the client wants global alignment; naming that trade-off is consultant-grade.
